# How to network and find more DM clients

Internal working doc. Not published.

`marketing-campaign.md` decides **which channels** to use. This doc is the
human layer underneath it: how to actually talk to people, who to talk to, what
to say, when to ask, and how a conversation becomes a consultation request.
Channels don't book evenings. People introducing people book evenings.

Same honesty rule as everything else — no invented testimonials, credentials,
capacity, or numbers.

---

## 1. The math that makes this easy

Run the numbers before building any outreach plan.

```
evenings_needed_per_year = target_revenue ÷ price_per_evening
hosts_needed = evenings_needed ÷ evenings_per_host_per_year
```

Fill those in with real figures and the result is startling every time: this
business does not need an audience. It needs **a small number of the right
hosts, some of whom rebook** (see `how-to-dm-dnd-for-fun-and-profit.md` §4).

That reframes the whole activity. You are not doing marketing at scale, you are
building a referral network dense enough to keep a capped calendar full.
Depth over reach, always. Twenty people who can vividly describe what you do
outperform two thousand who vaguely saw a post.

**Practical consequence:** every hour spent on reach-shaped activity (follower
counts, broad posting, ad experiments) is an hour not spent on the two motions
that actually work — guests becoming hosts, and hosts introducing hosts.

## 2. Your inventory: who already exists

Before any outreach, do this once, on paper, in an hour. It is the highest-
yield thing in this doc and it feels too simple to bother with.

List, by name:

- **People who host.** Not people you like — people who *throw things*. Dinner
  parties, game nights, birthdays, holiday gatherings, book clubs. The single
  best predictor of a Storyscape client is that someone already hosts and cares
  about it going well.
- **Connectors.** People who know everyone and enjoy that role. They don't have
  to be prospects. One connector is worth a dozen prospects.
- **People with a reason this year.** A milestone birthday, a reunion, a
  retirement, a move, a group of friends who keep saying they never see each
  other.
- **People new to the area.** Newcomers host more than settled residents — they
  are actively building a social circle (`marketing-campaign.md` names the
  corporate relocation stream).
- **Adjacent professionals.** Anyone already selling into "I'm hosting
  something and want it to be good": private chefs, event photographers,
  florists, caterers, party rentals, realtors, concierge services.

Then sort into three tiers: **talk to now**, **talk to when there's a reason**,
**not a fit**. Work the first tier only. Most people skip this and spend months
on strangers while a warm list sits unworked.

Keep it in the same spreadsheet as the inquiry log. One row per person. No CRM.

## 3. How to describe it in a normal conversation

The most common way this business loses a prospect is a bad answer to "so what
do you do?" Have three versions ready, sized to the moment.

**The one-liner** (someone at a party, low interest, moving on):
> "I run private tabletop adventure nights in people's homes — like a dinner
> party where I bring the whole story."

**The two-sentence** (they leaned in):
> "Someone hosts friends at their place, and I build an adventure around that
> specific group — their personalities, the occasion, who's played before and
> who hasn't — then guide the whole evening. The host gets to actually sit down
> and be part of it instead of running everything."

**The "who's it for"** (they're deciding whether it's for them):
> "It works best for close groups, mixed experience levels — some people who
> know the hobby, some who've never touched a character sheet. That mix is the
> point, not a problem."

Rules for all three:

- **Lead with the evening, not the hobby.** "D&D" pre-sorts listeners into fans
  and people who think it isn't for them. Hospitality framing doesn't.
- **Name the host's relief.** The buyer isn't purchasing a game, they're
  purchasing not having to produce one. "You host the people, I host the world"
  is the whole pitch compressed.
- **Never pitch.** State what it is, then stop talking. The interested person
  asks the next question; that's the signal. Pitching past it is what makes the
  conversation feel like a sale and kills referral willingness.
- **Have the next step ready and small.** Not "want to book?" — "I'll send you
  the link, it explains it better than I do." The site does the selling
  (`content/how-it-works/`), which is exactly what it was built for.

## 4. The highest-yield motion nobody runs: guests become hosts

Every booked evening puts you in a room with a handful of pre-qualified
prospects who are *experiencing the product* rather than hearing about it.
They already know the host, so they're inside the trust network. They've seen
exactly what they'd be buying. There is no colder-to-warmer conversion path
available anywhere else in this business.

Most DM-for-hire operations treat guests as an audience. They're the pipeline.

How to work it without ever selling at the table:

- **Be excellent to every guest, individually.** The quiet guest who never got
  a moment is the guest who never books (§6 of the profit doc — everyone acts,
  nobody spectates). Table craft *is* the sales motion.
- **Let them ask.** Some guest always asks how this works or what it costs.
  Answer honestly, briefly, and hand them the site. Don't expand it into a
  pitch in front of the host — that converts the host's gift into your
  advertisement and it's felt immediately.
- **Capture nothing at the table.** No cards fanned out, no signup, no QR code
  on the snack table. It breaks the evening's frame, which is the product.
- **Let the host be the bridge afterward.** The follow-up note to the host
  (§5) is where guest interest gets picked up: "If anyone at the table asked
  about doing one of their own, feel free to send them my way."

Expect the lag to be long. A guest who books is often booking six months later,
for their own birthday, with a group you've never met. Track it anyway —
"referral source per booking" exists to make this visible.

## 5. The referral ask, done properly

`marketing-campaign.md` correctly ranks the post-event referral ask as the
highest-converting channel. Here's the mechanic, because it's easy to do badly
and one awkward ask costs the relationship.

**Timing:** in the follow-up, within a day or two of the evening — while the
feeling is live, after the evening has ended (never during it).

**Specificity is everything.** "Let me know if you hear of anyone" produces
nothing, because it asks the person to do a search. A named, bounded ask
produces introductions:

> "Is there one other group you think would enjoy this? Happy to be
> introduced, and no pressure at all if nothing comes to mind."

One group. Explicit permission to decline. That's it.

**Make the introduction free.** The referrer should have to do one thing: send
a link or forward a note. Have a short, plain paragraph they can copy — not
marketing copy, something a friend would actually send. Every unit of effort
you push onto a referrer halves the chance it happens.

**Ask for the other two things at the same time:**

- Permission for a photo or a quote (the site has no proof yet and this is how
  it earns real proof instead of a fabricated one).
- Their honest note on what worked and what didn't — the answer feeds §3 of the
  profit doc, and asking makes people feel like collaborators rather than
  customers, which is itself referral fuel.

**Thank referrers, and close the loop.** Tell them when their introduction
booked. People refer again when they learn their referral mattered. Skip cash
incentives at this scale — they convert a gift into a transaction and cheapen a
premium, trust-based service.

## 6. Node people: partnerships that are worth the conversation

A node is anyone who repeatedly stands next to your buyer at the moment they're
planning something. One good node beats a hundred strangers.

Ranked by fit for this specific business:

1. **Local game/hobby stores and board game cafes.** Audience is pre-sold on
   the format. Best ask: a single demo or a table at an event, not a permanent
   card rack nobody looks at.
2. **Private chefs and caterers working NWA homes.** Same buyer, same price
   band, same evening, non-competing. A chef who's already booked for a dinner
   party is standing exactly where you want to be standing. This is the
   strongest untapped node on the list.
3. **Event photographers and party rental/décor people.** Same logic, they see
   hosting plans early.
4. **Corporate relocation contacts, HR/community managers, newcomer groups.**
   A steady stream of people building a social life from zero.
5. **Realtors working relocations.** They hand new arrivals a "how to live
   here" list. Being on it costs one coffee.
6. **Librarians, school/teacher networks, youth and community programs.** Lower
   budget, but dense with connectors and milestone occasions.

**How to approach a node.** Not "will you refer me." Bring something first:
run a free demo table at their event, offer the cafe a slow-night activity,
give the chef a partner their client will thank them for. The ask, when it
comes, should be a single sentence and easy to say no to.

**What to avoid:** anything with a commission structure, exclusivity, or a
contract. At this volume the administrative cost exceeds the return, and it
makes a hospitality relationship feel like an affiliate deal.

## 7. Showing up locally without becoming a salesperson

- **Do one thing well and repeatedly** rather than five things once. A monthly
  presence at a single game store or a recurring community event builds
  recognition; scattered one-offs build nothing.
- **Demo differently than you deliver.** A convention or store table is
  ninety minutes, pregenerated characters, high-clarity, designed to end early
  and leave them wanting more. Don't run a shortened version of the real
  product — run an advertisement that happens to be genuinely fun.
- **Volunteer facilitation is legitimate business development,** but cap it.
  Two or three a year. Beyond that it competes with paid evenings for the same
  scarce hours (§1 of the profit doc).
- **Go where hosts are, not only where gamers are.** Gamers are the adjacent
  audience; hosts are the buyer. A neighborhood association, a newcomers'
  mixer, a supper club — the buyer density is higher than at a game night.

## 8. Digital, sized correctly for a local service

Three things, and genuinely nothing else until the calendar is full:

1. **Google Business Profile**, accurate and claimed. This is how "private D&D
   Bentonville" and "in-home entertainment Northwest Arkansas" reach you. Free,
   set up once.
2. **One link that does the explaining.** The site already is this. The whole
   digital job is making sure the link is easy to send and lands somewhere that
   answers the obvious questions. That's `/how-it-works/` and `/questions/`.
3. **A slow, honest cadence of real material.** Prep photos, table setup, a
   map. It exists so someone who was told about you has something to look at,
   not to build an audience. One post when there's real material beats a
   schedule you manufacture content to fill.

Skip: ads, content marketing, lead magnets, an email funnel. All of them solve
a demand problem this business doesn't have.

## 9. Qualifying, because a bad booking costs a slot

Not every interested person should become a commission. The consultation is
already positioned as discovery (`content/how-it-works/` — "discovery, not a
disguised checkout"), so use it as a qualifying step.

Good-fit signals: they're hosting for a reason; guests are a close group;
mixed experience is framed as exciting rather than a problem; they care about
the evening being good; they ask about the people, not the rules.

Walk-away signals: price is the first and only question; they want a generic
cheap one-shot; the group is far larger than you can facilitate well; half the
guests don't want to play; the date is inside your lead-time floor; the space
can't seat the game.

Declining well is also networking. "This isn't quite the right fit, but here's
what might be" leaves a person who still refers you. A bad booking takes a
slot, produces no referral, and is the only kind of evening that damages the
network.

## 10. Staying warm without becoming noise

Most of the pipeline is people who are interested but not hosting *right now*.
That's normal — hosting is occasion-driven. The job is to still be top of mind
when their occasion arrives.

- **Occasional availability notes** to past inquiries and past hosts, only when
  dates genuinely open. Honest scarcity, not manufactured urgency.
- **Seasonal timing beats random timing.** Holidays, birthdays you know about,
  the weeks people plan reunions. One well-timed note to the right person
  outperforms a newsletter.
- **Non-commercial contact counts.** Congratulating someone, sending something
  they'd like, showing up at their thing. This is the part people call
  "networking" and skip because it isn't measurable. It's most of it.
- **Rule of thumb:** if a message has no reason to exist other than reminding
  someone you exist, don't send it.

## 11. The trap

Networking is the most comfortable possible form of procrastination. It feels
productive, it's social, it's infinitely expandable, and it has no ship date —
the same mechanism as Trap 2 in `ryan-instincts.md`, wearing friendlier
clothes. Building a contact spreadsheet, drafting outreach templates, and
researching partnership structures can absorb unlimited weeks while zero
evenings get booked.

The correction is the same: **the only outputs that count are conversations
held and evenings run.** If a week produced neither, no amount of network
infrastructure happened.

And the counterpart trap: the business that fills its calendar by referral
alone and never builds a network is one rough season away from silence. The
network is maintained *while* things are good, which is exactly when it feels
unnecessary.

---

## 12. The weekly practice

Small enough to actually do. Everything above collapses into this:

- **One conversation a week** with someone on the tier-one list — no agenda,
  no pitch.
- **One ask after every commission** — one group, plus photo/quote permission.
- **One node contact a month** — a chef, a store, a community group.
- **One spreadsheet, updated after each of the above.** Name, how you know
  them, last contact, what they'd need for it to be the right time.

---

## The short version

You need a few dozen right people, not an audience. The guests at every evening
are the warmest prospects you will ever have, so table craft is the sales
motion. Ask one specific referral question after every commission and make
saying yes take ten seconds. Be the person a private chef recommends. And count
only conversations and evenings — everything else is preparation for
networking, not networking.

# How to DM D&D for fun and profit

Internal working doc. Not published. `docs/pricing.md` covers how to land on a
rate; `docs/marketing-campaign.md` covers how to fill the calendar. This one
covers the part in between: how the business actually makes money once people
are booking, and what makes it good instead of merely busy.

Same honesty rule as the site — no invented numbers here. Every figure below is
a variable to fill in from real commissions, not a claim.

---

## 1. The one economic fact

**The constraint is hours, not demand.**

One person, evenings only, in one metro. Realistically that is a small number
of commissions a month before quality degrades — and quality *is* the product,
because referral is the entire acquisition channel (`marketing-campaign.md`).

Everything follows from this. A demand-constrained business grows by selling
more. An hours-constrained business grows by making each hour worth more. Every
lever below is one of exactly three:

1. **Fewer hours per evening** (without the guests feeling it)
2. **More revenue per evening** (price, scope, guest band)
3. **More evenings per relationship** (the one nobody uses, and the best one)

Anything that isn't one of those three is a hobby expense wearing a business
costume.

---

## 2. The unit of profit is the evening, not the month

Track a real P&L per commission. Not estimated — timed.

```
evening_margin = price
               − (consult_hours + prep_hours + travel_hours + table_hours
                  + followup_hours) × target_hourly
               − hard_costs (printing, minis, consumables, mileage)
```

Two things fall out of the first three commissions you measure honestly:

- **`prep_hours` dominates.** The table hours are the visible product. Prep is
  where the evening is actually made and where the margin is actually lost. A
  four-hour evening with sixteen hours of prep behind it is a real risk, not a
  hypothetical one — and it's invisible until it's measured.
- **`prep_hours` has enormous variance.** The same nominal evening can take 6
  hours or 20 depending on how much was built from scratch. That variance, not
  the average, is the thing to manage.

So: **time every commission.** Four buckets — consult, prep, table, follow-up.
A note on your phone is sufficient tooling. This is the only measurement in the
business that changes decisions.

---

## 3. Lever one — cut prep hours without cutting the bespoke feeling

This is the central craft problem of the business, and the one most
DM-for-hire operations get backwards.

### What guests actually experience as "made for us"

Very little of the prep is what creates the feeling. Guests register
personalization from a small number of high-salience touches:

- Their own names, in-jokes, professions, or history surfacing in the fiction
- A character that fits how that specific person likes to play (or how they're
  nervous about playing)
- The occasion being acknowledged inside the story, not just around it
- The tone matching the room — the consultation's actual payload

They do **not** register: encounter balance math, a fully original setting
bible, custom stat blocks, or a map drawn from zero. That work is felt only as
"this was good," which a reused skeleton delivers identically.

### The structural answer: a fixed chassis, a bespoke skin

Build a small number of reusable evening **shapes** — not adventures, shapes.
Something like the three directions already on `/how-it-works/` (First-Timer's
Journey, Puzzle Box, Epic Continuation). Each shape is a durable skeleton:
opening hook slot, three-to-four scene beats, an escalation, a decision point,
a closing image. Pacing, rules-teaching moments, and facilitation cues live in
the shape and never get rebuilt.

The bespoke work then lands where it's visible: names, motivations, the
specific problem this group gets handed, the one object or image that belongs
to this table. Reuse the invisible, custom-build the visible.

Realistic effect: the gap between a from-scratch evening and a reskinned one is
the single largest margin variable in the business. Measure it — it will
justify the chassis work immediately or tell you the chassis is over-built.

### The trap on the other side of this

There is a strong pull to turn "a few reusable shapes" into a system: a setting
bible, a modular encounter library, a prep pipeline, a template repo. That is
Trap 1 in `ryan-instincts.md` — paying franchise setup costs for something that
runs a handful of times — and Trap 4: documentation that costs more to retrieve
than to hold in your head.

The discipline: **a shape earns existence by being used twice.** Write the
skeleton down only after the second commission that used it, and write it as
one page. Nothing gets built for a hypothetical future commission. If a prep
tool is being built while no date is on the calendar, that's Trap 2 (the
toolchain as buffer), not preparation.

### Physical inventory compounds, so buy it once

Minis, terrain, a good playlist set, handout stock, a travel case, a set of
reusable prop objects. Every one of these is a fixed cost that amortizes across
every future evening and reduces both prep time and setup time. This is the one
category where spending money genuinely buys back hours — as long as it's
bought against evenings already booked, not in anticipation of them.

## 4. Lever two — revenue per relationship is the actual profit engine

The second evening with the same group is dramatically cheaper to produce than
the first, and it's the part of the model that almost nobody exploits.

On a repeat commission you already have: the guests, their play styles, the
table dynamics, the physical logistics of the house, the established fiction,
and a relationship that needs no re-selling. Consultation collapses. Setup is
known. Prep starts from a running world instead of a blank page.

The site already leaves the door open — the Experience page names "a possible
next chapter when the table wants to continue." **Treat that as a product
line, not a courtesy.**

### How to make continuation happen without selling at the table

Instinct 20 in the vault — *leave the session at a fork, not a forced close* —
is a business mechanism disguised as a craft rule. A satisfying arc that closes
one question and opens another gives the host a reason to book again that comes
from the story rather than from you. It costs nothing at the table and it is
the difference between a one-off and a relationship.

Design for it explicitly:

- Every evening ends resolved **and** with one live thread
- The follow-up note (the one that already carries the referral ask) mentions
  the thread once, plainly, and then drops it
- Have an honest answer ready for "could we do this again" — scope, lead time,
  and what changes second time around

### Why this matters more than more bookings

A host who books three chapters a year is worth three new-client acquisitions
at a fraction of the prep cost and *zero* acquisition cost. In an
hours-constrained business, a returning table is the highest-margin hour
available. If the calendar has to choose between a new inquiry and a returning
table at the same price, the returning table is the better sale almost every
time — and the referral value doesn't drop, because repeat hosts are the ones
who recommend you hardest.

## 5. Lever three — price integrity

Covered in `pricing.md`; the operational half is short.

- **Scope is confirmed before the date is held, in writing.** The process page
  already promises this ("nothing important should be left to a surprise
  invoice"). It's also margin protection: unbounded scope is unbounded prep.
- **A deposit converts prep time into paid time.** Prep starts before the
  evening; payment should too.
- **Never discount to fill a date.** In a referral business, the first price a
  host hears becomes the price they quote to their friends. A discount doesn't
  cost one booking's margin, it resets the anchor for the entire network the
  booking came from.
- **Raise the rate when the calendar fills without hesitation.** That's not
  opportunism; in an hours-constrained business it's the only available form of
  growth.

## 6. The craft that makes people refer you

Referral is the acquisition channel, so table craft is a growth function.
What produces the "you have to book this" conversation is specific and
repeatable.

**Every guest acts, nobody spectates.** The vault's rule 6 — the main character
must act, not watch — applies per-guest at a table. The quiet guest who never
got a moment is the guest who doesn't recommend the evening. Track attention
deliberately during the session; give each person at least one moment the story
turned on.

**The new player has to feel competent within fifteen minutes.** Mixed-
experience tables are the stated target market. The failure mode is the
first-timer who spends the evening feeling behind. Front-load one low-stakes
choice they can't get wrong, then one they can.

**The threat has rules** (vault rule 9). Guests trust an evening where the
danger is legible and consequences are consistent. Vague menace reads as
improvisation, which reads as unprepared, which is the one thing a paid
facilitator can never look like.

**The host gets to be a guest.** This is the entire promise — "you host the
people, I host the world." The operational version: don't ask the host to
fetch, explain, manage timing, or handle other guests. Bring everything. If the
host ends up working, the core promise failed no matter how good the story was.

**The ending is engineered.** Guests remember the last ten minutes with
disproportionate weight. A deliberate closing image, a named callback to
something planted early (vault rule 11 — plant it before you pay it off), a
clean stopping point. This is the cheapest high-leverage prep in the whole
business.

**Photos exist and permission is asked.** The marketing plan needs real
material and the site currently has no proof. One good photo and one earned
quote per commission, asked for once, politely, after the evening.

## 7. Capacity design — protect the thing being sold

Burnout is the realistic failure mode here, not lack of demand. The vault's
burst-window rule (work happens in 2–4 week high-intensity pushes, and that's a
spec, not a flaw) argues against a smooth, always-on booking calendar.

Practical shape:

- **Season the year.** Define booking windows and closed windows in advance,
  and say so honestly — the site's capacity-bound positioning is already built
  for this and it's true rather than a scarcity trick.
- **Cap commissions per week, hard.** Two evenings in a weekend with full prep
  behind both is how quality drops; the second one is the one that doesn't get
  referred.
- **Keep a lead-time floor.** Prep quality is the product. An evening booked
  inside the floor either gets a rushed skeleton or eats the week. Neither is
  worth the fee. (`README.md` still flags lead time as undecided — decide it.)
- **Batch the domain.** Trap 3, context-switching tax: a week that mixes prep
  with unrelated technical work loses hours to reloading. Prep clusters in the
  days before an evening, not in scattered evenings across a fortnight.

## 8. Failure modes, named

- **Scope creep dressed as generosity.** "I'll just also make custom character
  art / a soundtrack / physical handouts for each guest." Unpaid hours that
  become the new expected baseline for every future commission.
- **The setting bible.** Real work, no ship date, grows forever. Vault rule 5
  flags it explicitly as a warning sign rather than progress.
- **Underpriced first bookings to friends.** They're also the seed of the
  referral network, so they set the network's price expectation permanently.
- **Saying yes to the wrong table.** A group that wants a cheap generic
  one-shot, a party too large to facilitate well, a room that can't seat the
  game, an occasion where half the guests don't want to be playing. Each one
  costs a full commission slot and produces no referral. The consultation is a
  qualifying step; use it as one.
- **Quality drift under volume.** The first evening that's merely fine is
  invisible in the short term and expensive later — it's a referral that never
  happens, which shows up as "marketing stopped working" months afterward.
- **Confusing the craft hobby with the business.** Building for the joy of
  building is entirely legitimate; it just isn't margin, and it shouldn't be
  counted as work when the calendar is being planned.

## 9. Ways to scale, ranked by whether they're worth it

**Worth it:**

1. **Repeat chapters with existing tables** (§4). Highest margin hour available.
2. **The reusable chassis** (§3). Raises margin on every future evening.
3. **Raising the rate.** The only growth an hours-constrained solo business gets
   for free.
4. **Corporate/team commissions, selectively.** Higher budget, tolerates a
   larger group, and one booking can produce several private referrals. Costs:
   different facilitation skill, daytime availability, a different price shape.
   Test it once before committing to it.

**Probably not worth it:**

5. **Multi-table events / festivals.** Different business, different skills,
   thin margin per guest, no referral density.
6. **Selling the prepared adventures as products.** A product business
   masquerading as a lever. It competes for the same hours, sells into a
   crowded market at a low price point, and none of the current positioning
   transfers.
7. **Hiring other DMs.** This is where the model breaks. The product is a
   specific person's facilitation and preparation; the brand cannot survive a
   variable-quality second practitioner, and supervising one costs the hours
   the expansion was supposed to buy. Refuse this one on purpose, not by
   default.

## 10. The scoreboard

Six numbers. A spreadsheet, monthly, no tooling.

| Number | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Prep hours per commission | The margin variable that actually moves |
| Margin per evening | The only unit of profit in the business |
| Repeat rate (tables booking a second chapter) | The highest-leverage lever |
| Inquiry → booking conversion | Whether the price and the consultation are working |
| Referral source per booking | Which relationships compound |
| Evenings run per month vs. the cap | The early-warning signal for quality drift |

If prep hours are falling and repeat rate is rising, the business is improving
even if booking count is flat. If booking count is rising while those two are
flat, it's just getting busier.

---

## The short version

Sell fewer, better evenings at a price that respects the preparation. Reuse the
invisible structure and custom-build the visible details. End every evening at
a fork so the best customer you'll ever have is the one already sitting at the
table. Measure prep hours, because that's where the business is either working
or quietly losing money.
